There are plenty of variables a developer can move: list price, the renders, which portals it advertises on, how much it spends on the launch campaign. The one with the highest return and the least measurement is how long it takes to reply.

## Why it matters so much

Someone enquiring about a project almost never enquires about one. They message three or four ads in the same session, almost always from a phone, almost always in time stolen from the day. What happens next is simple: whoever replies first gets a conversation, and whoever gets a conversation books the showroom visit.

It isn't that the first reply always wins. It's that the first reply gets to define what the conversation is about: which layout, which down payment, which plan. The ones arriving later answer against a frame another project already built.

That's why response time isn't a customer service metric. It's a commercial one, and in most launches it predicts conversion better than anything you can do with the campaign itself. It's also where the cost of not measuring shows most: every lead costs media money, and a lead that goes cold because nobody replied is spend thrown away twice.

## How to measure it without fooling yourself

Three mistakes make the number most developers have useless.

**The average.** An average of forty minutes can be ten enquiries answered in two minutes and one answered the next day. What matters is the median and the ninetieth percentile. The ninetieth percentile is what tells you how many enquiries are being lost entirely.

**Business hours only.** Measuring only nine to six erases exactly the problem. A large share of enquiries arrive outside working hours, because that's when people browse projects. If the report excludes them, the report says everything is fine.

**The portal's or the agency's number.** Portals report response time on their own definition, which usually starts counting when the salesperson opens the enquiry, not when it arrived. The media agency reports leads delivered, which is something else again. Useful for comparing against other advertisers; useless for knowing how long a person waits.

The useful measurement is ugly and manual: export two weeks of conversations per channel, and for each enquiry subtract the timestamp of the first inbound message from the first outbound one. By the end, the table builds itself.

That empty table is the first deliverable of any diagnostic we run in the sector, and it almost always surprises management. The worst-performing channel is rarely the one the team suspects, and during a launch it's usually the one getting the most spend.

## The three real causes of delay

When you look at where the time goes, it's almost never negligence.

**Nobody owns the channel.** Portal enquiries land in a shared inbox everyone looks at and nobody works. Instagram messages are seen by whoever runs social, who isn't in sales. The enquiry isn't lost for lack of people: it's lost for lack of an owner.

**Replying properly takes three screens.** To answer seriously you have to open the unit spreadsheet, verify the layout is still available and at what price, and check the showroom calendar. Nobody does that from a phone at ten at night, so the reply gets postponed until they're at a computer — and by then twelve hours have passed.

**The launch spike.** That's when half the year's volume arrives and the sales team is the same size it always was. Hiring doesn't fix it: nobody hires four people for two weeks, and by the time they were trained the spike would be over.

## What can be automated and what can't

What automates well is exactly the first response and the three screens: recognizing which project and layout they asked about, verifying availability and current pricing, answering the specific question and offering two slots from the showroom's real calendar.

What doesn't automate is everything after. The visit, the payment-plan negotiation, reading the person: that's the salesperson's work and it's where the sale is decided. The system's goal isn't to replace them, it's to have them arrive at that conversation with the visit already booked instead of with forty unanswered messages.

And there's a case where automating subtracts: the ultra-high-ticket product with twenty units sold through referrals, where the personal relationship from the first message is the product. Delay costs there too, but the fix is organizational, not technical.

## Where to start tomorrow

Before any project, two things cost nothing and move the number:

Assign an owner and hours to each channel, in writing, including what happens on launch weekend. It sounds bureaucratic and it resolves a meaningful part of the problem.

Measure two weeks. Not to justify a purchase, but because the discussion about what to automate is a completely different conversation with a table on the desk.

After that, if the ninetieth percentile is still in hours and the volume justifies it, the conversation about automating the first response becomes concrete: you know how many enquiries are at stake, on which channel, at what hour, and how much spend brought them in.
