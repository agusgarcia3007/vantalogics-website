There are plenty of variables a real estate agency can move: price, photography, which portals it publishes on, how it trains its agents. The one with the highest return and the least measurement is how long it takes to reply.

## Why it matters so much

Someone enquiring about a property almost never enquires about one. They message three or four listings in the same session, almost always from a phone, almost always in time stolen from the day. What happens next is simple: whoever replies first gets a conversation, and whoever gets a conversation books a viewing.

It isn't that the first reply always wins. It's that the first reply gets to define what the conversation is about: which area, which budget, which options. The ones arriving later answer against a frame somebody else built.

That's why response time isn't a customer service metric. It's a commercial one, and in most portfolios it predicts conversion better than anything you can do with the inventory itself.

## How to measure it without fooling yourself

Three mistakes make the number most agencies have useless.

**The average.** An average of forty minutes can be ten enquiries answered in two minutes and one answered the next day. What matters is the median and the ninetieth percentile. The ninetieth percentile is what tells you how many enquiries are being lost entirely.

**Business hours only.** Measuring only nine to six erases exactly the problem. A large share of property enquiries arrive outside working hours, because that's when people browse. If the report excludes them, the report says everything is fine.

**The portal's number.** Portals report response time on their own definition, which usually starts counting when the agent opens the enquiry, not when it arrived. Useful for comparing against other agencies on that portal; useless for knowing how long a person waits.

The useful measurement is ugly and manual: export two weeks of conversations per channel, and for each enquiry subtract the timestamp of the first inbound message from the first outbound one. By the end, the table builds itself.

## The three real causes of delay

When you look at where the time goes, it's almost never negligence.

**Nobody owns the channel.** Portal enquiries land in a shared inbox everyone looks at and nobody works. Instagram messages are seen by whoever runs social, who isn't in sales. The enquiry isn't lost for lack of people: it's lost for lack of an owner.

**Replying properly takes three screens.** To answer seriously you have to open the CRM record, verify the property is still available and check the calendar. Nobody does that from a phone at ten at night, so the reply gets postponed until they're at a computer — and by then twelve hours have passed.

**Nights and weekends.** That's when a good share of the volume arrives and when nobody is there. Hiring doesn't fix it: a person covers forty of the week's one hundred and sixty-eight hours.

## What can be automated and what can't

What automates well is exactly the first response and the three screens: recognizing which property they asked about, verifying availability against the CRM, answering the specific question and offering two slots from the agent's real calendar.

What doesn't automate is everything after. The viewing, the negotiation, reading the person: that's the agent's work and it's where the deal is decided. The system's goal isn't to replace them, it's to have them arrive at that conversation with the viewing already booked instead of with forty unanswered messages.

And there's a case where automating subtracts: the premium operation with a ten-property portfolio, where the personal relationship from the first message is the product. Delay costs there too, but the fix is organizational, not technical.

## Where to start tomorrow

Before any project, two things cost nothing and move the number:

Assign an owner and hours to each channel, in writing, including what happens at the weekend. It sounds bureaucratic and it resolves a meaningful part of the problem.

Measure two weeks. Not to justify a purchase, but because the discussion about what to automate is a completely different conversation with a table on the desk.

After that, if the ninetieth percentile is still in hours and the volume justifies it, the conversation about automating the first response becomes concrete: you know how many enquiries are at stake, on which channel, and at what hour.
